    How to choose a surf school

    A surf lesson is only as good as the school running it. Before you pay, check the beach, the group size, who is teaching, and whether the business is actually operating — not just that it has a booking button.

    Start with the beach, not the brand

    The right school is the one that teaches on a beach that suits you that week. A beginner wants a sandy, reasonably sheltered beach break with a lifeguard in season. An improver wants somewhere with a bit more push. If a school only works one beach, check that beach actually works for your level — tide, wind and swell change the same stretch of sand from hour to hour.

    On Surflist, every school sits inside a town. Open the town hub first — for example Newquay or Croyde — then the surf schools in that place. The town page is the useful starting point: it shows who actually teaches there, not who advertises the widest.

    Check they are a real, operating business

    Surflist only lists a school when we can confirm it from the business's own official website. That is the bar for being on the directory. It is not a review, and it is not the same as the verified badge.

    • Open the school's own site from the listing. If the site is gone, the socials are abandoned, or the only presence is a third-party booking widget, be cautious.
    • Match the name, town and beach. A school that says it is in one place and photographs another is a warning sign.
    • Look for a real address, a phone number you can call, and instructors named as people rather than a stock photo.

    Who is teaching, and how many of you are there?

    Ask who will be in the water with you. Qualifications vary by country — ISA, Surfing England, and national coaching awards are common on the listings we carry — but the useful question is whether the school publishes them. A page that names the head coach and the ratio is more useful than a page that says "highly qualified team".

    Group size matters more than most people think. A two-hour lesson with eight people in the water is a different experience from a private or a family group. If you are nervous, travelling with children, or short on days, pay for a smaller ratio. The surf school listings show the levels each school says they teach — beginner, intermediate, advanced, kids — so you can filter before you click through.

    Kit, changing, and what is actually included

    A lesson price that includes a wetsuit and a soft-top board is the normal setup for a first session. Confirm it. Cold-water destinations need a thicker suit than a tropical beach break; if you run cold, ask about sizes and whether they have boots and gloves in winter.

    Changing rooms, showers and a place to lock a bag sound minor until you are in a car park in the wind. Schools based on the sand or in a beachfront centre are simply easier on a first trip. Those details live on each school's Surflist page when we have them, and on the school's own site when we don't.

    Kids, mixed abilities, and one booking for the whole family

    Minimum ages are not consistent. Some group lessons start at eight; private lessons sometimes go younger if an adult is in the water. Read the school's rule, not a forum post. If one person in the group is a complete beginner and another already surfs, a family private is usually kinder than putting everyone in the same group.

    Price is a clue, not the decision

    Cheap lessons exist. So do expensive ones that are just a busier beach. Compare what you get: hours in the water, ratio, kit, and whether you can come back the next day if the first session is blown out. Multi-day courses and "surf and stay" packages only make sense if the forecast and the beach will still work for you on day two.

    How to use Surflist to shortlist

    1. Pick the town you are actually staying in, or a beach you can reach without a long drive.
    2. Open that town's surf-schools page and filter by level.
    3. Read the listing page — blurb, spots, lessons, prices when we have them — then click through to the official site to book.
    4. If two schools look equal, choose the one whose beach and hours match your week, not the one with the louder photos.

    If you run a school and you are not on the directory yet, get listed. We check you against your own site before you appear.

    FAQs

    Do I need to be able to swim?

    Most schools ask you to swim a set distance — often 25 or 50 metres — without a board. Check the listing and the school's own site before you book; the requirement is theirs, not Surflist's.

    What's a sensible group size?

    Look for a published instructor-to-student ratio. Eight to one is a common upper limit for group lessons. Smaller groups cost more and usually mean more time on your own waves.

    Does a Surflist verified badge mean a school is better?

    No. Verified is a paid listing state. Every surf school on Surflist already has its own page. Use the listing, the official website, and the details you care about — not the badge — to choose.
