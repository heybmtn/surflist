The Ultimate Guide to Surfing in Popoyo (2026): Best Seasons, Surf Spots & Water Temperatures

We first came to Popoyo chasing the wave that put Nicaragua on the map, and we ended up staying far longer than we planned. That happens a lot here. It's a dusty little surf village on the Pacific coast, with one of the most reliable reef breaks in Central America right off the sand, warm water all year, and sunsets that make you forget how sore your arms are. This guide covers everything we wish we'd known before our first trip — the best seasons, the surf spots by skill level, the water temperatures month by month, and what to pack. We'll be honest about the trade-offs too: the crowds on the reef, the dust in the dry months, and the long, bumpy road in. Popoyo isn't polished. That's the point.

A short, punchy coastline with a lot packed in

Popoyo sits on Nicaragua's Emerald Coast (Costa Esmeralda), in the Tola district of Rivas. It isn't a big region — the core surf zone runs along a single sandy beach, Playa Guasacate and Playa Santana, broken up by a few shelves of reef.

That compactness is the magic. Within about a half-mile stretch, a complete beginner, an improving intermediate and a big-wave charger can all have the best session of their trip. Add the boat breaks up and down the coast, and you've got real variety without much driving.

Waves all year round

Nicaragua is known as the land of eternal offshores, and Popoyo lives up to it. The wind blows across nearby Lake Nicaragua and grooms the waves clean for over 300 days a year.

The short version of the seasons: the green (wet) season from May to October brings the biggest, most powerful swells, while the dry, windy season from November to April brings smaller, cleaner waves and blue skies. Popoyo's Main Reef works in both.

Where the swell comes from

The best surf here is generated far away, deep in the Southern Hemisphere. From roughly May to October, long-period south and south-west groundswells march up the Pacific and hit Popoyo's reefs with real punch.

In the dry season the dominant swell shifts to smaller, shorter-period pulses from other directions, which is why the waves back off but stay surfable. What stays constant is the offshore wind — that Lake Nicaragua effect keeps mornings clean almost year-round.

Suits (almost) every skill level

Popoyo is honestly best for confident intermediates and up. The Main Reef is powerful and crowded, and the Outer Reef is for experts only.

But there's a genuinely good beginner spot right in town, plenty of mellow peaks on the smaller days, and a longboard-friendly point when the size is right. And if you want easier learning waves, the beaches around San Juan del Sur to the south are some of the friendliest in the country. We'll cover all of it below. Browse surf schools in Popoyo if you want a lesson.

Climate and lifestyle

This is the tropics, close to the equator, so days and nights stay a similar length all year and it's warm whenever you visit. The dry season is sunny and dusty; the green season is lush, humid and punctuated by afternoon downpours.

Between sessions there isn't a huge amount to do, and we mean that as a compliment. You'll wander the beach, read in a hammock, cool off in the Guasacate tidal pools at low tide (the swell is too rough for easy swimming), eat fresh fish, and watch the sun drop into the Pacific. It's a place to slow down.

Surf culture

Popoyo went global in 2015, when the reef hosted an International Surfing Association world championship. Since then, surf camps and lodges have steadily filled in along the beach, but the town has kept its rough, end-of-the-road feel.

English is less common here than in touristy Costa Rica, so a little Spanish goes a long way. Most travellers find Nicaraguans warm and welcoming, and the line-up, while competitive at the Main Reef, is generally friendly.

Getting there

Most people fly into Augusto C. Sandino International Airport in Managua (MGA). From there it's roughly a 2-hour drive (about 100 kilometres / 63 miles) south to Popoyo — easiest by pre-arranged shuttle or a hired 4×4, as the last stretch is rough.

There's also a small local airport, Costa Esmeralda (ECI), just 20–30 minutes from town, with short hops from Managua and from San José and Liberia in Costa Rica. It's handy but flights are limited, so check schedules before you build a trip around it.

Coming from Costa Rica, you can fly into Liberia (LIR), cross the border at Peñas Blancas, and drive up — around 2.5 to 3 hours to Popoyo depending on the crossing. Budget travellers can take local buses ("chicken buses") via Rivas and Las Salinas; they're cheap but slow and involve a few transfers.

Surfing Popoyo by season

The "best" season depends entirely on what you're after. Want the biggest, most powerful waves and don't mind rain? Come in the green season. Want cleaner, smaller, more forgiving waves under blue skies? Come in the dry season.

Because Nicaragua sits so close to the equator, it doesn't have the four temperate seasons you might be used to. It has two, defined by rainfall rather than temperature — so we've split this section that way.

☀️ Dry and windy season (November–April)

Best for: beginners and improving intermediates, longboarders, and anyone who wants clean, sunny, manageable waves.

This is the mellower, more welcoming face of Popoyo. The rain all but stops, the skies go blue, and strong offshore winds — especially in January and February — hold the waves clean well into the day.

Swells are smaller and more spaced out. On quiet days you'll get waist-to-chest surf; on the better days it's head-high, with the odd bigger set sneaking through. It's a great window to work on technique without being thrown into survival mode, and the Main Reef becomes a friendlier place to learn to read a reef wave.

The trade-off: those same strong offshores can make the early water feel a touch cooler, and Semana Santa (Holy Week, late March into early April) is the busiest week of the year, with lots of local and regional surfers in town.

Average conditions

  • Water temperature: 25–26°C (77–79°F)
  • Air temperature: 30–32°C (86–90°F)
  • Recommended wetsuit: boardshorts or swimsuit; an optional 2 mm top for chilly, windy dawn sessions in January and February

Best spots

  • Beginner's Bay (learners)
  • Main Reef (on smaller, cleaner days)
  • Stoneys / La Lejana (longboarding)

🌊 Green and swell season (May–October)

Best for: confident intermediates and advanced surfers chasing size, power and barrels.

This is when Popoyo truly shines. South and south-west groundswells fire up the reefs, full rivers form clean sandbars, and the whole coast lights up. July and August are usually the most reliable months for size — head-high to well overhead is standard, and bigger pulses push through on the strongest swells. The waves get hollow, and the Main Reef throws proper barrels.

It's also the rainy season, so expect humid days and heavy afternoon or evening downpours. The upside: everything turns green and lush, and the crowds thin out compared with the Semana Santa peak.

Be realistic about your level here. When the Outer Reef switches on, this is expert territory. Early intermediates may find the bigger days more than they bargained for — but there's still plenty of manageable surf on the smaller peaks in between.

  • Air temperature: 30–31°C (86–88°F)
  • Recommended wetsuit: boardshorts or swimsuit; a rash vest for comfort on longer sessions

  • Main Reef (barrels)
  • Outer Reef (experts only)
  • Manzanillo and Playgrounds (boat breaks)

April and November sit in between and can offer the best of both, if you time a swell right.

Popoyo's water temperatures throughout the year

Here's the good news: you won't need a proper wetsuit. Popoyo's water sits in the tropics and stays warm all year, so boardshorts or a swimsuit and a rash vest do the job in almost every month.

The one nuance is those strong dry-season offshores. In January and February the wind can pull cooler water up to the surface and add a bit of chill on dawn patrols — nothing dramatic, but some surfers like a thin neoprene top for the early session.

Is the water cold?

Not really — and if you've surfed anywhere in Europe, it'll feel like a warm bath. Compared with cold-water trips like Cornwall or Portugal, where you're zipped into a 4/3 half the year, Popoyo is a treat: you paddle out in shorts and stay out until your arms give up rather than until you get cold.

The only thing to keep an eye on is that dry-season upwelling. It's a comfort thing, not a survival thing — a rash vest or thin top covers it.

The best surf spots in Popoyo

Popoyo packs an unusual amount of variety into a small area. You've got a world-class reef machine, a genuine beginner bay, forgiving points for longboarders, and a scatter of boat breaks nearby that only a handful of people surf on any given day.

Because everything is close together, we've grouped the spots by how you reach them rather than by region: the breaks you can walk to, the ones you take a boat or 4×4 to, and a road trip south for easier waves and a change of scene.

Zone 1: The walk-to breaks (Playa Guasacate and Playa Santana)

This is the heart of it. A single sandy beach, split by reef, hosting almost everything Popoyo is famous for — all within a short stroll of the surf camps.

Main Reef (Popoyo)

The star. A powerful A-frame that breaks over a rock shelf, offering rippable walls and hollow sections both left and right. It works across all tides, most swell directions and almost any wind, which is why it's the most consistent wave in Nicaragua — it breaks from around 2 feet up to 20 feet.

The catch is the crowd. It's one shifty peak and one of the busiest waves in the country, so you'll be sharing and competing for sets. It's powerful and breaks over reef, so it isn't a beginner wave.

Best for:

  • Confident intermediates and advanced surfers
  • Anyone hunting a reliable barrel
  • Surfers happy to work for waves in a busy line-up

Outer Reef

The monster. A Pipeline-style slab that only wakes up on big swells (roughly 12 feet and over), a short paddle out from the Main Reef. When it's on, it's heavy, shallow and unforgiving — probably the most serious wave in Nicaragua.

This is strictly experts and big-wave surfers only. For everyone else, it's a spectacular show to watch from the beach.

Best for:

  • Experienced big-wave surfers only

Beginner's Bay

Exactly what the name promises. Tucked in behind the picturesque promontory of Mag Rock (Magnific Rock) at the south end of Guasacate, the reef shelters the bay and softens the waves into mellow, rolling lines with a left and a middle section.

It's the best place in town to learn — though on bigger swells it can pick up size, so it's not always a first-timer's wave. Check it on the day. See Popoyo surf schools.

Best for:

  • Beginners and early intermediates
  • Longboarders on the smaller days

Stoneys / La Lejana

Sitting between the Main Reef and the Outer Reef, this is a softer, more forgiving point-style wave (you'll also hear it called Rubber Duckies or Cobra). It needs a bit of size to work, but when it does it peels nicely and gives longboarders and improving surfers a break from the Main Reef crowd.

Best for:

  • Longboarders
  • Intermediates wanting a gentler, less crowded option

Who should visit Zone 1?

  • Intermediate and advanced surfers who want a world-class reef on their doorstep
  • Beginners, thanks to sheltered Beginner's Bay
  • Longboarders looking for forgiving points on the right day
  • Anyone who values walking to the surf over long drives

Zone 2: The boat and 4×4 breaks nearby

When the Main Reef gets busy, or you just want to explore, a short boat ride or drive opens up quieter, high-quality waves up and down the Emerald Coast. Most surf camps can arrange boats.

Manzanillo

A long, mellow left point that wraps around a beautiful cliff-backed bay, reached by a quick boat ride (or through the Guacalito/Mukul resort). On a good south-west swell the rides stretch 200–300 metres, mostly open-faced and carvable with one barrelling section partway down. Watch the rocks.

Ideal for:

  • Intermediates and up who love a long left
  • Surfers wanting a scenic, less-crowded session

Playgrounds

A boat-only, deepwater reef off the Chacocente Wildlife Refuge. Its horseshoe shape amplifies any swell, so it's often the biggest wave around and can jump to double or triple overhead fast. On smaller days it's mellow enough for confident intermediates, but respect the size, the currents and the long paddle.

Ideal for:

  • Advanced surfers on bigger days
  • Confident intermediates on smaller, calmer ones

Panga Drops and Colorado (Hacienda Iguana)

A short drive away inside the Hacienda Iguana community are two more serious waves. Panga Drops is one of the most consistent breaks in the country and usually holds more size. Colorado is a hollow, punchy beach break that serves up some of the best barrels in Central America — advanced surfers only.

Ideal for:

  • Advanced surfers chasing barrels and consistency

Who should visit Zone 2?

  • Intermediates and advanced surfers wanting to escape the Main Reef crowd
  • Surfers happy to arrange a boat or a 4×4
  • Barrel hunters (Colorado, Playgrounds on their day)

Zone 3: The road trip south (San Juan del Sur and around)

If you want easier learning waves, a change of scene, or a bit more nightlife, point the car south. The San Juan del Sur surf zone is roughly 1.5 hours away and is where Nicaragua is at its most beginner-friendly.

Playa Maderas

Our pick of the south. A shapely A-frame with peeling rights and frothier lefts, it's genuinely great for learning yet still fires for better surfers on the bigger wet-season days. Add the jungle setting, howler monkeys at dawn and beach bars at dusk, and it's easy to see why people fall for it. Treat it as a day trip from San Juan del Sur.

Best for:

  • Beginners and improvers (surf schools on the beach)
  • Intermediates on the bigger days

Playa Gigante

A quiet fishing village with a calm bay, a couple of surf camps, and world-class breaks within reach — without the development of San Juan del Sur. Playa Amarillo is the main wave, with sandbanks along its length and better lefts nearer town. See surf in Playa Gigante.

Best for:

  • Surfers who want a laid-back base and empty line-ups

Playa El Coco and the San Juan beaches

About 30 minutes south of San Juan del Sur, Playa El Coco is a beautiful, usually empty beach with easy, small waves — ideal for complete beginners and longboarders. Closer to town, Playa Remanso and Playa Hermosa offer more gentle beginner and lower-intermediate options.

Best for:

  • First-timers and longboarders wanting the easiest waves around

Our personal favourite

It has to be Zone 1 — the walk-to breaks. Rolling out of a cabana and being in the water in minutes, then watching the Outer Reef detonate while you trade waves at the Main Reef, is a hard combination to beat. The honest downside is the crowd on the reef, especially in peak swell season. When it gets too busy, that's our cue to grab a boat out to Manzanillo and find some space.

The best beginner surf spots in Popoyo

Let's be straight: Popoyo is not the easiest place in Nicaragua to learn. The Main Reef is powerful and crowded, and much of the surf breaks over rock. But there is one genuinely good beginner spot in town, and easier options a short drive away — so learning here is very doable if you pick the right wave.

1. Beginner's Bay (Popoyo)

Best for: first-timers and early intermediates, sheltered from the heavier reefs.

Protected by Mag Rock, this is the safest place to learn in Popoyo itself, with mellow rolling waves and surf schools nearby. Just check the size on the day, because bigger swells reach in here too.

Why we recommend it:

  • Sheltered by the rock, so it's softer than the open beach
  • A left and a middle section to spread people out
  • Surf schools and board hire close at hand
  • Steps from town, no boat or long drive needed

2. Playa Maderas (San Juan del Sur zone)

Best for: beginners who want a friendly, sandy A-frame and don't mind the drive south.

Worth the 1.5-hour trip if learning is your priority. It's sandy, the waves aren't intimidating on a normal day, and cheap group lessons are easy to find right on the beach. See surf in San Juan del Sur.

Why we recommend it:

  • Sandy bottom and forgiving waves
  • Well set up for lessons and board hire
  • Fun, sociable, beginner-friendly crowd

3. Playa El Coco

Best for: complete beginners and longboarders wanting the easiest, quietest waves around.

Half an hour south of San Juan del Sur, El Coco is usually small, gentle and near-empty — a lovely, low-pressure place to find your feet.

Why we recommend it:

  • Small, easy waves most days
  • Rarely crowded
  • A beautiful beach to spend the whole day on

What makes a good beginner surf beach?

When we size up a spot for learners, we look for a sandy bottom, or a reef that shelters rather than threatens. Gentle, rolling waves rather than steep, hollow ones. Consistency, so you're not waiting around. Space to spread out, away from the fastest, most competitive peaks. And good surf schools and board hire nearby. Beginner's Bay ticks most of these in Popoyo itself; Maderas and El Coco tick all of them down south.

Should beginners surf in the green season?

If you're just starting, the dry, windy season (November to April) is the easier time to learn — smaller, cleaner waves and sunny skies. The green season (May to October) brings more power and size, which is exactly what you don't want as a first-timer.

If you do come in the green season as a beginner, stick to the sheltered and southern spots, go early before the wind and crowds build, and don't be shy about booking a lesson to fast-track your progress safely.

Popoyo surf trip packing list

Warm water and reef breaks shape what you bring. The headline: pack light on neoprene, but don't skip the reef booties.

🏄 Surf essentials

Surfboard

Bring a board with a bit of paddle power for the reef, plus something you can throw around on the smaller days. Beginners are better off hiring locally or joining a surf school than lugging a soft-top across borders. If you're chasing the green-season barrels, a step-up or a sturdier shortboard earns its place.

Wetsuit

Honestly, you barely need one. Boardshorts or a swimsuit work all year. Pack a rash vest for comfort and chafe on long sessions, and if you feel the cold, a thin 2 mm top for windy dry-season dawns.

Surf wax

Bring tropical (warm-water) wax — the water rarely drops below 25°C (77°F), so anything cooler-rated will melt off your board.

Reef booties

Not optional here. Much of Popoyo breaks over sharp rock, and booties save your feet on the reef and the rocky entries and exits. We'd call these the single most useful thing to pack.

Changing poncho / towel

Handy for the dusty car park and quick changes between sessions.

Useful extras

  • Reusable water bottle (it's hot, and you'll dehydrate fast)
  • Dry bag for wet gear and valuables on boat trips
  • Waterproof phone pouch
  • Small first aid kit, with plenty for reef cuts and antiseptic
  • Earplugs (surfer's ear is real after a week of dawn patrols)
  • Ding repair kit
  • Flip-flops / sandals
  • Snorkel and mask for flat days and the tidal pools
  • A little cash in córdobas and some basic Spanish

The best Popoyo trip isn't necessarily the one with the biggest swell on the forecast. It's the one where you read the day honestly, surf the wave that matches your level, and let the place set the pace — a dawn session at the reef, a boat out to a quiet point, an afternoon in the tidal pools, and a sunset you'll still be thinking about long after you've flown home. Pack light, bring your booties, learn a few words of Spanish, and stay a little longer than you planned. That's the Popoyo way.

FAQs

Is Popoyo good for beginner surfers?

It can be, but it's not the easiest place in Nicaragua to learn. The Main Reef is powerful and crowded, so beginners should stick to sheltered Beginner's Bay in town, or make the trip south to the friendlier, sandy waves around San Juan del Sur, like Playa Maderas and Playa El Coco.

What is the best time of year to surf in Popoyo?

For size and power, May to October — with July and August the most reliable for big, hollow waves. For cleaner, smaller, more forgiving conditions under blue skies, November to April. February and March are lovely for learning; June to September are best for experienced surfers.

Can you surf in Popoyo all year round?

Yes. The Main Reef is famous for being one of the most consistent waves in Central America, and Popoyo gets over 300 days of offshore winds a year. The size and character change with the season, but there's almost always something to ride.

Do I need a wetsuit in Popoyo?

No — the water stays warm all year, between about 25°C and 28°C (77–82°F), so boardshorts or a swimsuit and a rash vest are plenty. The only exception is windy dawn sessions in January and February, when some surfers like a thin 2 mm top.

Is the Main Reef or Beginner's Bay better for me?

It depends on your level. The Main Reef is a powerful, crowded reef wave for confident intermediates and up. Beginner's Bay, sheltered by Mag Rock just down the beach, is far more forgiving and the right choice for anyone still learning.

Are the boat breaks worth it?

If you're an intermediate or better, yes. A short boat ride to Manzanillo or Playgrounds gets you away from the Main Reef crowd and onto quieter, high-quality waves. Most surf camps can arrange the boat, and it's one of the best things about basing yourself here.

Can you longboard in Popoyo?

Yes, best in the smaller dry-season months. The Main Reef offers a longboard-friendly wall on the right day, and softer point-style waves like Stoneys / La Lejana and the mellow days at Beginner's Bay suit longboarders well. It's not a classic small-wave longboard paradise, but it works.

Is surfing in Popoyo expensive?

No — Nicaragua is one of the most affordable surf destinations in Central America. Accommodation, food and lessons all cost noticeably less than in neighbouring Costa Rica, which is a big part of Popoyo's appeal. The main costs are getting there and any boat trips you add on.

Is Popoyo a good destination for a surf road trip?

It's a great base for one. From Popoyo you can explore the boat breaks up and down the Emerald Coast, then drive south to the San Juan del Sur zone for Maderas, Gigante and the easier beaches. A hired 4×4 opens up the most, given the rough coastal roads.

Anything I should know before I go?

A few practical things: the coastal roads are rough, so a 4×4 or a pre-booked shuttle beats a standard taxi. Amenities are basic — Popoyo has a small clinic, with Rivas about an hour away for anything more serious. English is less common than in Costa Rica, so learn a little Spanish. And as with any trip, it's worth checking your government's current travel advice before you book.
