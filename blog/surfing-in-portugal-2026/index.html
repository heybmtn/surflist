    The Ultimate Guide to Surfing in Portugal (2026): Best Seasons, Surf Spots & Water Temperatures

    We've surfed up and down Portugal's coast for years. We've been to the cold, empty beaches north of Porto and the warm, easy waves of the Algarve. This guide covers the best time to visit, the spots we love in each region, water temperatures month by month, the right wetsuit for each season, and what to pack. Whether it's your first lesson or your fiftieth trip, we'll help you find the right wave.

    Portugal is the most complete surf spot in Europe, in our view. It has about 850 kilometres (530 miles) of coastline. There are waves nearly every day of the year. The water is warmer than most of northern Europe. And the country is a joy to explore between surfs. We've spent many early mornings here watching the sun come up over an empty line-up. We still find new corners to check every trip.

    It's also a land of contrasts, and most guides skip over this. The water in the north can be six or seven degrees colder than the water down south on the same day. Nazaré has the biggest waves on the planet, and it sits just an hour up the road from the gentle beginner beaches of Peniche.

    That variety is why Portugal works for everyone. But it also means where and when you go really matters. Let's look at what makes the place special.

    A coast made for finding waves

    Portugal's west coast faces the open Atlantic along its whole edge. The sea floor drops away quickly near the shore, so waves arrive with plenty of power. The coast has lots of shape too: points, reefs, river mouths, long beaches and sheltered coves, all close together. This means there's nearly always something worth surfing if you're willing to move around.

    That's the key to a Portugal surf trip. When the wind turns bad at one beach, a spot 20 minutes away is often clean. We plan our trips around that freedom, not around one single beach.

    Waves all year round

    Portugal gets waves in every season, and each one feels different. Winter brings the biggest, most powerful surf, and the famous Nazaré giants. Autumn is the sweet spot, with steady swells and water that still holds some summer warmth. Spring cleans up as the winter storms fade and the crowds thin out. Summer is the smallest, gentlest and warmest, which is why it's the best time to learn.

    Where the waves come from

    The surf here comes from big Atlantic swells. Storms far out in the ocean push waves onto a coast that faces straight into them. Because the sea floor is steep near the shore, the waves keep their power right up to the beach. Nazaré is the extreme case. A deep underwater canyon there points swell straight at Praia do Norte and makes it huge. In 2011, American surfer Garrett McNamara rode a wave there about 24 metres (78 feet) high. On the biggest days the waves top 20 metres (65 feet).

    Waves for every level

    This is where Portugal really shines. Total beginners get some of Europe's best learning beaches. They are sandy, gentle and full of surf schools. Improvers get steady beach breaks and easy points to practise on. Better surfers get world-class reefs, points, heavy barrels and, if they're brave, the biggest waves on Earth. Longboarders are looked after too, with plenty of long, peeling waves on the smaller days.

    The weather and life off the water

    Portugal is warm, sunny and relaxed. The outdoor season is long, and the food is great and cheap. A normal day might go like this: a surf at dawn, a long lunch of grilled fish and a glass of local wine, another surf when the wind drops, then a custard tart on the way home. Prices are low for Western Europe. A fresh seafood meal for about €15 is normal. That makes longer trips easy to afford.

    Surf culture

    Surfing is part of life on this coast, and the towns show it. Ericeira is a small white fishing town that became a World Surfing Reserve. Surf schools sit right next to working fishing boats. Peniche is simple, friendly and full of surf camps. The best spots can get busy and a bit competitive. But the wider scene is warm and welcoming, and the country puts real money into the sport.

    Getting there

    Three main airports serve mainland Portugal. Which one you pick shapes your trip.

    Lisbon (LIS) is the biggest and the best all-rounder. It's about 45 minutes to Ericeira, around 1.5 hours to Peniche, and about 1 hour 20 to Nazaré. The city's own beaches are close by too.

    Porto (OPO) is your way into the colder, wilder, quieter north. Matosinhos is barely 20 minutes from the airport.

    Faro (FAO) drops you into the Algarve. From there you can reach the warm south coast and the wild west coast.

    Cheap flights reach all three from across the UK and Europe. We really suggest renting a car. The coast rewards wandering, and buses and trains don't reach most of the good waves.

    Surfing Portugal by season

    There's no single "best" time to surf Portugal. It all depends on what you want. Learning, and want warm water and small waves? Come in summer. Chasing big waves and empty line-ups, and don't mind the cold? Come in winter. Want the best all-round mix? Come in autumn. Here's how each season looks.

    🌸 Spring (March–May)

    Best for: improving beginners and intermediates who want smaller waves, quiet beaches, and don't mind cool water.

    Spring is quietly one of the best times to come. The big winter swells slowly settle into something easier to handle. The weather warms up. And the beaches are still empty before the summer rush. The water is at its coldest in February and March, then slowly warms through April and May.

    Spring is a season of change, so conditions shift a lot. Early spring can still bring winter-sized surf. Late spring feels almost like summer down south. For improvers who want steady waves without a crowd, it's hard to beat.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 14–17°C (57–63°F)
    • Air temperature: 15–22°C (59–72°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 4/3mm, moving to 3/2mm by late spring

    Best regions

    • Peniche and the Silver Coast
    • Ericeira
    • The Algarve (warmer water, both coasts working)

    ☀️ Summer (June–August)

    Best for: beginners, and anyone who wants warm water, small waves and a lively scene.

    Summer is learning season. The swells are small and gentle, the water is at its warmest, and the surf schools are busy. If you've never stood on a board, this is the time to come.

    Now for the honest part. The west coast can go flat for days at a time. The popular beaches get busy, and surf schools pack the water in the mornings. Better surfers often find the mainland too quiet in high summer. They either chase the odd swell in the Algarve or take the season off. There's also the north wind, called the nortada. It blows onshore on many west-coast beaches by early afternoon. So dawn surfs are your friend.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 16–20°C (61–68°F) on the central and Lisbon coast; up to 22–23°C (72–73°F) in the Algarve
    • Air temperature: 22–30°C (72–86°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 3/2mm; a shorty or boardshorts in the Algarve on warm days

    Best regions

    • The Algarve (warmest water, sheltered beginner beaches on the south coast)
    • Peniche and Baleal (beginner central)
    • Lisbon's beaches (easy to reach, small summer waves)

    🍂 Autumn (September–November)

    Best for: everyone — the best all-round season, from improving beginners to advanced surfers.

    If we could only surf Portugal in one season, we'd pick autumn. September is magic. The water is at its warmest of the year after the long summer. The summer crowds have thinned out. And the first proper swells of the season start to roll in. The waves build through October and November into something with real power.

    Early autumn suits every level. By November the swells are bigger, and better for confident improvers and advanced surfers. Nazaré's big-wave season is also starting to stir. This is the season we suggest to almost everyone.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 15–20°C (59–68°F), warmest in September
    • Air temperature: 17–26°C (63–79°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 3/2mm early, 4/3mm by November

    Best regions

    • Ericeira
    • Peniche and Nazaré
    • The Algarve (warm water lasts well into October)

    ❄️ Winter (December–February)

    Best for: advanced surfers chasing size, big-wave watchers, and anyone who loves an empty line-up.

    Winter is when Portugal shows its full power. This is Supertubos firing, Nazaré heaving, magazine-cover season. The swells are the biggest and steadiest of the year. And apart from the big-wave circus at Nazaré, most line-ups are lovely and quiet.

    Be honest with yourself about your level, though. Much of the west coast gets heavy and serious in winter. Cold water and wind make sessions hard work. But the reward is huge. Score a clean winter day at a good reef with only a few surfers out, and you'll see why we keep coming back. Even if you don't paddle out, standing at the Nazaré lighthouse and watching giants break is something you won't forget.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 13–16°C (55–61°F)
    • Air temperature: 11–17°C (52–63°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 4/3mm plus 3mm boots; a 5/3mm and gloves in the north for longer surfs

    Best regions

    • Nazaré (for watching; experts only in the water)
    • Peniche (Supertubos on the right day)
    • The Algarve's south coast (the mildest winter option on the mainland)

Improvers get steady waves. And advanced surfers get more power week by week. If you're a pure beginner set on the warmest, gentlest water, come in July. If you're an experienced surfer chasing size, aim for November to February.

    Portugal's water temperatures throughout the year

    Portugal's water is colder than most first-time visitors expect, and there's a good reason. The coast sits in the path of cool Atlantic currents. In summer, something called upwelling makes it colder still. North winds push the warm surface water out to sea, and cold water rises up from the deep to take its place. This can drop the sea by several degrees, sometimes overnight. That's why the air can be 30°C while the sea sits at 16°C.

    Is the water cold?

    Compared to warm-water spots, yes. But it's easy to handle. Think of the Portuguese mainland as a step warmer than the UK, France or Ireland, and much warmer than Scandinavia. A 3/2mm suit covers most of the year on the central coast. You'll only really want a 4/3mm and boots in the depths of winter. If you feel the cold, go up a millimetre. You'll last far longer in the water.

    Regional differences worth knowing

    The gap between north and south is big. The Algarve is the warm exception on the mainland. The sheltered south coast around Tavira can hit 22–25°C (72–77°F) in August and September. That's warm enough for boardshorts. The north (Porto, Viana do Castelo) is the coldest. It rarely climbs much above 18–19°C even in its warmest month, and summer upwelling hits it hardest. The central coast and Lisbon sit in between. If warm water matters to you, head south. If you don't mind the chill and want empty waves, the north delivers.

    The best surf spots in Portugal

    Portugal's waves come in every shape: gentle sandy beach breaks, world-class reefs, heavy barrels and river-mouth points. So we'll go region by region. Here are the areas we know best, roughly north to south through the surf heartland, then out to the two ends of the country.

    Ericeira — Europe's first World Surfing Reserve

    Ericeira is just 45 minutes north of Lisbon. It's a small white fishing town with an amazing number of good waves. In 2011 it became the first World Surfing Reserve in Europe. That status protects a run of world-class reefs and points packed into about eight kilometres of coast. It's our favourite base for a trip that leans towards improvers and advanced surfers. But there's plenty here for beginners too.

    Ribeira d'Ilhas

    This is the reserve's most famous and easiest wave to reach. It's a long, peeling right-hand point, and it hosts pro contests. It works on all tides and lots of swell sizes. The wave is long, so surfers spread out along it and you can still get plenty of rides.

    Best for:

    • Intermediates and advanced surfers
    • Longboarders on the smaller, gentler days
    • Beginners on very small days (with a school)

    Foz do Lizandro

    This is a wide, sandy beach at a river mouth, five minutes south of town. Most surf schools bring their students here. It's gentle, easy and works often.

    Ideal for:

    • Beginners and first-timers
    • Longboarders
    • Families

    Coxos

    Coxos is a world-class, powerful right-hand point that breaks over shallow reef. It's one of the heaviest waves in the reserve, and a dream wave for those who can surf it. Be honest about your level here.

    Best for:

    • Advanced surfers with real barrel experience only

    Who should visit Ericeira?

    • Improvers ready to move onto reefs and points
    • Advanced surfers after world-class, steady waves
    • Beginners happy to learn at Foz do Lizandro and São Julião
    • Anyone who wants a proper surf town with great food and an easy Lisbon link

    Peniche and the Silver Coast

    If Portugal has a surf capital, it's Peniche. This fishing town sits on a hook-shaped bit of land, so it catches swell from almost every direction. That means there's nearly always something to surf somewhere. It's the heart of Portugal's surf-camp scene. Best of all, it has waves for total beginners and for the world's best, all within a 20-minute drive.

    Baleal

    Baleal is a curved bay tucked behind an island, sheltered from the biggest swells. The beach works like a sliding scale. Head north for the gentlest waves, or drift south for more size and power. It's one of Europe's great beginner spots, which is why the surf camps gather here. See surf schools in Baleal.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and improvers
    • Longboarders
    • Families

    Supertubos

    This is the "Portuguese Pipeline". It's a fast, hollow, barrelling beach break, and it hosts the world tour. You'll see it on plenty of magazine covers. It's powerful and it doesn't forgive mistakes. It works best on a west swell with a light offshore wind.

    Best for:

    • Advanced surfers with barrel experience only

    Nazaré (Praia do Norte)

    Nazaré is 40 minutes north, and it needs little introduction. An underwater canyon here makes the biggest rideable waves on the planet. For almost everyone, this is a spot to watch, not surf. Head to the lighthouse between October and March for the show. On smaller days, the town beach has more normal waves.

    Best for:

    • Big-wave experts (Praia do Norte)
    • Everyone else, as watchers

    Who should visit Peniche and the Silver Coast?

    • Beginners wanting the best learning beaches and surf camps in the country
    • Advanced surfers chasing barrels at Supertubos
    • Anyone who wants to see Nazaré's giants
    • Surfers who like steady waves — something is nearly always working here

    The Lisbon coast

    You can be surfing within half an hour of landing at Lisbon. That makes the city's coast great for a short surf trip or a first taste of Portugal. The waves split across two areas: the Cascais line to the west of the city, and Costa da Caparica to the south across the river.

    Carcavelos

    This is the closest beach to central Lisbon. You can reach it by train in about 20 minutes. It's a steady, sandy beach break that works in lots of conditions, and it has a lively scene with plenty of schools. It's the obvious first stop. See surf schools in Carcavelos.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and intermediates
    • Surfers without a car

    Costa da Caparica

    This is a long stretch of coast south of the city, with dozens of peaks over more than 20 kilometres of sand. You can nearly always find a bank that's working and get away from the crowd. Locals and surf camps both love it. See surf schools in Costa da Caparica.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and improvers
    • Anyone wanting variety and space

    Guincho

    Guincho is near Cascais and fully open to the Atlantic, so it brings power and wind. It's a beautiful, wild beach that's hosted world contests. But the currents and gusts make it one for confident surfers, not first lessons.

    Best for:

    • Intermediate and advanced surfers
    • Windsurfers and kitesurfers

    Who should visit the Lisbon coast?

    • Beginners wanting the easiest access in the country
    • City-break travellers who want to mix surf and Lisbon
    • Surfers using buses and trains
    • Anyone chasing variety on a short, well-linked coast

    The Algarve

    Portugal's sunny south has a secret weapon: two coasts that face different ways. So there's nearly always a sheltered option, whatever the wind and swell are doing. The wild, west-facing Costa Vicentina runs north from Sagres and takes the full Atlantic. It's powerful, quiet and dramatic. The south coast around Lagos and Sagres is more sheltered, warmer and gentler. It comes into its own in summer, when the west coast goes flat. It's also the warm-water part of the mainland.

    Praia do Amado

    This is one of the west coast's steadiest and most beginner-friendly beaches. It has schools on the sand and gentle waves for every level. The setting is wild and lovely, inside a nature park.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and intermediates
    • Surf-school lessons

    Praia da Arrifana

    Arrifana sits below tall cliffs near Aljezur. It has a sheltered bay that's great for learning, plus a punchy right-hand point for the more experienced. It's one of the best all-round spots on the west coast.

    Ideal for:

    • Beginners in the bay
    • Intermediates and advanced surfers on the point

    Sagres (Tonel, Beliche, Mareta)

    The tip of Portugal's south-west corner catches both west and south swells. That makes it the region's most reliable surf hub. Tonel and Beliche face the Atlantic. Sheltered Mareta faces south, and it's a gentle option when everywhere else is too big. See surf schools in Sagres.

    Best for:

    • All levels, depending on the beach and the conditions
    • Wave-hunters who like options within a short drive

    Who should visit the Algarve?

    • Beginners wanting warm water and sheltered summer beaches
    • Intermediate and advanced surfers chasing the wild west coast in autumn and winter
    • Anyone mixing surf with a proper holiday
    • Surfers who want the freedom of two coasts

    The North (Porto, the Silver Coast and Costa Verde)

    The north is Portugal's wild, cold, quiet corner, and we love it. The water is colder. The surf is a bit wilder and harder to predict. And the line-ups are the emptiest on the mainland. Fly into Porto and you can surf within 20 minutes at Matosinhos. Then explore the wild beaches that run south to Espinho and north towards the Spanish border.

    Matosinhos

    This is Porto's main city beach, and one of the best places in Europe to learn. A big harbour wall shelters it from the north wind. So it's often surfable when the rest of the coast is stormy. And it's barely 20 minutes from the airport. See surf schools in Matosinhos.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and improvers
    • Quick weekend or city-break surf trips

    Espinho

    Espinho is the north's best-known surf town, 20 kilometres south of Porto. It's easy to reach by car or train, and it has a punchy beach break that hosts pro-junior and longboard events. Much of the beach works for beginners on smaller days.

    Best for:

    • Beginners to intermediates
    • Surfers based in Porto without a car

    Viana do Castelo (Cabedelo)

    Viana do Castelo is a handsome town up towards the Spanish border. Its Cabedelo beach break is sheltered by a long pier and runs well for much of the year. It has a strong local scene, and it draws wind- and kitesurfers too.

    Best for:

    • Improving beginners and intermediates
    • Anyone wanting empty waves and a real local feel

    Who should visit the North?

    • Beginners learning at Matosinhos or Espinho
    • Improvers who want space and don't mind cold water
    • City-break surfers using Porto as a base
    • Anyone tired of crowds and after a wilder, more real trip

    Our personal favourite

    We keep coming back to Ericeira. There are so many good waves in one walkable town. The Lisbon link is easy. And you can surf a gentle beach break in the morning, then watch the pros at Coxos in the afternoon. The honest trade-off is crowds. The reserve is famous, so the best spots get busy, and the reefs aren't for beginners. If we wanted warm water and a holiday feel, we'd pick the Algarve for its two coasts. If we wanted peace and quiet, we'd point the car north. But for an all-round Portugal trip, Ericeira is the one.

    The best beginner surf spots in Portugal

    Portugal is one of the best places in the world to learn to surf. It has long sandy beaches, gentle summer waves, warm-ish water and loads of surf schools. So there's an easy wave for almost anyone. These are the spots we'd send a first-timer to.

    1. Baleal (Peniche)

    Best for: total beginners and first lessons.

    This is the gold standard for learning in Portugal. It's sheltered, sandy and gentle. The bay works as a sliding scale of size. And it has more surf schools and camps than almost anywhere else in the country. Browse Baleal surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Sheltered from the biggest swells
    • Sandy bottom and gentle, rolling waves
    • Huge choice of surf schools and camps
    • Steady enough to surf most days

    2. Carcavelos (Lisbon)

    Best for: beginners who want easy access from the city.

    This is the easiest beginner beach to reach in the country. It's a 20-minute train ride from central Lisbon. It's a wide sandy break, and there are schools right on the sand. See Carcavelos surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Reachable without a car
    • Steady, gentle beach break
    • Loads of schools and rentals
    • Perfect for a first surf on a city break

    3. Costa da Caparica (Lisbon)

    Best for: beginners wanting space and variety.

    This is a huge stretch of beach with dozens of peaks. You can always find a quieter, gentler bank away from the crowd. See Costa da Caparica surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Long, sandy and steady
    • Plenty of room to spread out
    • Strong surf-camp scene
    • Great for beginners on the right banks, especially early morning

    4. Foz do Lizandro (Ericeira)

    Best for: beginners who also want a great surf town.

    This is a sandy river-mouth beach, and it's the main teaching spot in the World Surfing Reserve. It has gentle waves, with world-class breaks a short drive away for when you improve. See Ericeira surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Sandy bottom and gentle waves
    • Surf schools on the beach
    • Room to improve within the reserve
    • Lovely town right there

    5. Praia do Amado (Algarve)

    Best for: beginners wanting warm water and a wild setting.

    This is one of the west coast's steadiest and gentlest beaches. It has schools on the sand, a great beach bar and a stunning nature-park backdrop.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Steady, beginner-friendly beach breaks
    • Warmer water than the central and north coasts
    • Schools and rentals on site
    • Quiet, unspoiled surroundings

    6. Matosinhos (Porto)

    Best for: beginners on a Porto city break.

    This is Porto's main learning beach. A harbour wall shelters it, and it's just 20 minutes from the airport. It's often surfable when the rest of the north is blown out. See Matosinhos surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Sheltered from the north wind
    • Very easy to reach from the city
    • Well-known schools and rentals
    • Great for a quick weekend trip

    What makes a good beginner surf beach?

    When we judge a beach for first-timers, we look for a few things. We want a sandy bottom, with no reef or rocks to worry about. We want gentle, rolling waves, not steep or hollow ones. We want it to work often, so you're not sitting and waiting. We want space, so you can stay clear of other surfers. And we want good surf schools with trained teachers. The beaches above tick all those boxes.

    Should beginners surf in peak season?

    Summer (June to August) is the easiest time to learn. The waves are small and gentle, and the water is at its warmest. The trade-off is crowds. The popular beginner beaches fill up, mostly with surf-school groups in the mornings. Our tip: come in summer if it's your first time, but surf early. A dawn lesson, before the wind and the crowds, is calmer, cleaner and much more fun. Late spring and September are lovely, quieter options if you've got a little experience.

    Portugal surf trip packing list

    This list comes from years of our own trips. Here's what actually earns a place in the board bag.

    🏄 Surf essentials

    Surfboard

    If you're bringing your own, match it to the season. Summer's small, playful waves suit a groveller, fish or longboard. Autumn and winter's power calls for a step-up or your normal shortboard. Beginners are usually better off renting a soft-top when they arrive. Schools and shops in every surf town rent boards cheaply, and you save on airline board fees.

    Wetsuit

    This is the most important item. A 3/2mm covers most of the year on the central coast. Bring a 4/3mm and 3mm boots for winter and early spring. Bring a 5/3mm and gloves if you're heading to the cold north in deep winter. In the Algarve in high summer, a shorty or even boardshorts will do.

    Surf wax

    Match it to the water temperature. Use cool wax for winter and spring, cool-to-warm for autumn, and warm for high summer, especially in the Algarve. Bring more than you think. Wax is easy to forget and annoying to hunt down.

    Changing poncho / towel

    This is a lifesaver for getting changed in car parks and on windy beaches. It's much warmer than a normal towel between surfs on cold days.

    Useful extras

    • Reusable water bottle
    • Dry bag for wet wetsuits
    • Waterproof phone pouch
    • Small first aid kit
    • Earplugs (surfer's ear is a real risk in cold Atlantic water)
    • Ding repair kit
    • Flip-flops / sandals
    • Snorkel and mask for flat days

    Here's what years of trips here have taught us. The best surf trip to Portugal isn't the one with the biggest waves. It's the one where you stay flexible. Chase the clean corner when the wind turns. Take the lesson when it's small. Watch the giants when it's huge. And fill the flat days with long lunches, coast roads and cold swims. Come with a plan, but hold it loosely. Portugal always has a wave for you somewhere.

    FAQs

    Is Portugal good for beginner surfers?

    Yes, it's one of the best in the world. It has long sandy beaches, gentle summer waves and loads of surf schools. Spots like Baleal, Carcavelos, Costa da Caparica and Praia do Amado are great for learning. Come in summer for the easiest conditions.

    What is the best time of year to surf in Portugal?

    It depends on your level. Beginners should come in summer for small, warm, gentle waves. Advanced surfers want autumn and winter for size and power. For the best all-round mix, we'd pick September and October, with warm water, building swells and thinner crowds.

    Can you surf in Portugal all year round?

    Yes. Portugal gets Atlantic swell in every season. It's one of the most reliable coasts in Europe, with surfable waves on most days somewhere along the coast.

    Do I need a wetsuit in Portugal?

    Nearly always, yes. The Atlantic here is colder than many people expect. A 3/2mm suits most of the year on the central coast, with a 4/3mm and boots for winter. The Algarve in high summer is the one exception, where a shorty or boardshorts can be enough.

    Is the Algarve or the north better for surfing?

    They're very different trips. The Algarve has the warmest water, sheltered beginner beaches and a holiday feel, plus wild west-coast waves in winter. The north is colder and wilder, with the emptiest line-ups on the mainland. Pick the Algarve for warmth and comfort, and the north for peace and quiet.

    Is Ericeira good for surfing?

    Very. It's Europe's first World Surfing Reserve, with lots of world-class reefs and points in a small area, plus gentle learning beaches nearby. It leans towards improvers and advanced surfers, but there's something for everyone.

    Can you longboard in Portugal?

    Yes. There are plenty of long, peeling waves for logging, especially on the smaller days. Ribeira d'Ilhas in Ericeira, the gentler banks at Costa da Caparica and Carcavelos, and countless summer beach breaks all suit longboarders well.

    Is surfing in Portugal expensive?

    No, it's great value for Western Europe. Board and wetsuit rental is usually about €20–30 per day. Lessons and surf camps are cheap. And food and lodging cost little. A fresh seafood meal for about €15 is normal.

    Is Portugal a good place for a surf road trip?

    It's one of the best. The coast is compact and easy to drive, and it's packed with variety. You can surf a beginner beach, a world-class reef and a big-wave spot within a couple of hours' drive. Rent a car, follow the forecast, and let the wind decide where you surf each day.
