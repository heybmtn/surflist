    The Ultimate Guide to Surfing in Portugal (2026): Best Seasons, Surf Spots & Water Temperatures

    We've chased swells up and down Portugal's Atlantic coast for years — from the freezing, empty peaks north of Porto to the warm, forgiving rollers of the Algarve. This guide covers the best seasons to visit, the surf spots we keep coming back to region by region, month-by-month water temperatures, the right wetsuit for each time of year, and what to pack. Whether it's your first lesson or your fiftieth trip, we'll help you find the right wave.

    Portugal is, for our money, the most complete surf destination in Europe. Roughly 850 kilometres (530 miles) of Atlantic coastline, waves nearly every day of the year, warm-ish water by northern-European standards, and a country you'll genuinely want to explore between sessions. We've spent countless dawn paddle-outs here watching the light come up over an empty line-up, and we still haven't run out of new corners to check.

    It's also a country of contrasts, and that's the thing most guides gloss over. The water in Viana do Castelo can be six or seven degrees colder than the same-day sea in Tavira. Nazaré throws up the biggest rideable waves on the planet an hour up the road from the mellow beginner beaches of Peniche. That variety is exactly why Portugal works for everyone — but it also means where and when you go matters. Let's get into what makes the place special.

    A coastline built for finding waves

    Portugal's mainland faces the open Atlantic along its entire western edge, with a short continental shelf that lets swell arrive with plenty of punch. Add a genuinely indented coast — points, reefs, river mouths, long beach breaks and sheltered coves all within a short drive of one another — and you have a place where there's almost always something surfable if you're willing to move.

    That's the key to a Portuguese surf trip: mobility. When the wind swings onshore at one beach, a headland or a river mouth 20 minutes away is often clean. We plan trips around that flexibility rather than around a single spot.

    Waves all year round

    Portugal picks up swell in every season, and each has its own character. Winter brings the biggest, most powerful surf and the famous Nazaré giants. Autumn is the sweet spot — consistent groundswells with water still holding some summer warmth. Spring cleans up as the winter storms ease and the crowds thin. Summer is the gentlest, smallest and warmest, which is exactly why it's when most people learn.

    The swell source

    The surf here is driven by North Atlantic groundswells — low-pressure systems tracking across the ocean push long-period swell onto a coast that faces straight into it. Because the continental shelf is narrow, that energy doesn't dissipate before it reaches the beach. Nazaré takes this to an extreme: a five-kilometre-deep underwater canyon funnels and amplifies swell into the towering walls of Praia do Norte, where American surfer Garrett McNamara rode a roughly 24-metre (78-foot) wave in 2011 and put the town on the world map. On the biggest days the waves there exceed 20 metres (65 feet).

    Suits every skill level

    This is where Portugal really earns its reputation. Total beginners have some of Europe's best learning beaches — sandy-bottomed, gentle and dotted with surf schools. Intermediates get consistent beach breaks and forgiving points to progress on. Advanced surfers have world-class reef and point breaks, heavy barrels and, if they're brave enough, the biggest waves on earth. Longboarders are well looked after too, with plenty of long, peeling walls on the smaller days.

    Climate and life between sessions

    Portugal is warm, sunny and easy-going, with a long outdoor season and famously good, affordable food. A typical day might mean a dawn surf, a long lunch of grilled fish and a bottle of vinho verde, an afternoon session when the wind drops, and pastéis de nata on the way home. The cost of living is low by Western-European standards — a fresh seafood meal for around €15 is normal — which makes longer trips genuinely doable.

    Surf culture

    Surfing is woven into Portuguese coastal life, and the towns reflect it. Ericeira is a whitewashed fishing town turned World Surfing Reserve where surf schools sit alongside working fishing boats. Peniche is functional, friendly surf-camp central. The line-ups can get competitive at the famous spots, but the wider scene is welcoming, and there's serious investment in the sport at every level.

    Getting there

    Three main airports serve mainland Portugal, and which you fly into shapes your trip.

    Lisbon (LIS) is the largest and the best all-rounder — around 45 minutes to Ericeira, about 1.5 hours to Peniche, and roughly 1 hour 20 to Nazaré. It's also on the doorstep of the city's own beaches.

    Porto (OPO) is the gateway to the colder, wilder, less-crowded north — Matosinhos is barely 20 minutes from the terminal.

    Faro (FAO) drops you into the Algarve for the warm-water south coast and the wild western Costa Vicentina.

    Low-cost carriers fly into all three from across the UK and Europe. We'd strongly recommend renting a car: the coast rewards wandering, and public transport doesn't reach most of the good waves.

    Surfing Portugal by season

    There's no single "best" time to surf Portugal — it depends entirely on what you're after. Learning to surf and want warm water and small waves? Summer. Chasing size and empty line-ups, and don't mind the cold? Winter. Want the best all-round balance? Autumn. Here's how each season breaks down.

    🌸 Spring (March–May)

    Best for: improving beginners and intermediates who want size easing off, uncrowded line-ups, and don't mind cool water.

    Spring is quietly one of the best times to come. The heavy winter swells gradually settle into something more manageable, the weather warms up, and the beaches are still empty before the summer rush. Water temperatures are at their coldest of the year in February and March, then slowly climb through April and May.

    It's a transitional season, so conditions vary — early spring can still throw up proper winter-sized surf, while late spring feels almost summery on the south coast. For intermediates looking to progress on consistent waves without a crowd, it's hard to beat.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 14–17°C (57–63°F)
    • Air temperature: 15–22°C (59–72°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 4/3mm easing to 3/2mm by late spring

    Best regions

    • Peniche and the Silver Coast
    • Ericeira
    • The Algarve (warmer water, both coasts working)

    ☀️ Summer (June–August)

    Best for: beginners and anyone wanting warm water, small waves and a lively scene.

    Summer is learner season. The swells are at their smallest and most forgiving, the water is at its most comfortable, and the surf schools are in full swing. If you've never stood on a board, this is when to come.

    The trade-off is honesty time: the west coast can go genuinely flat for stretches, and the popular beaches get busy — surf schools pack the water in the mornings. Advanced surfers often find the mainland a bit lifeless in high summer and either chase the odd south-swell in the Algarve or simply take the season off. There's also the north wind (the nortada), which reliably blows onshore on many west-coast beaches by early afternoon, so dawn sessions are your friend.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 16–20°C (61–68°F) on the central/Lisbon coast; up to 22–23°C (72–73°F) in the Algarve
    • Air temperature: 22–30°C (72–86°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 3/2mm; a shorty or boardshorts in the Algarve on warm days

    Best regions

    • The Algarve (warmest water, sheltered south-coast beginner beaches)
    • Peniche / Baleal (beginner heartland)
    • Lisbon's beaches (easy access, small summer waves)

    🍂 Autumn (September–November)

    Best for: everyone — the best all-round season, from progressing beginners to advanced.

    If we could only surf Portugal in one season, it would be autumn. September in particular is magic: the water is at its warmest of the year after a long summer, the summer crowds have thinned, and the first proper groundswells of the season start rolling in. The waves build steadily through October and November into something with real power.

    Early autumn suits every level. By November the swells are bigger and more suited to confident intermediates and advanced surfers, and Nazaré's big-wave season is beginning to stir. It's the season we recommend to almost everyone.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 15–20°C (59–68°F), warmest in September
    • Air temperature: 17–26°C (63–79°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 3/2mm early, 4/3mm by November

    Best regions

    • Ericeira
    • Peniche and Nazaré
    • The Algarve (warm water lingers well into October)

    ❄️ Winter (December–February)

    Best for: advanced surfers chasing size, big-wave spectators, and anyone who loves an empty line-up.

    Winter is when Portugal shows its full power. This is Supertubos-firing, Nazaré-heaving, back-of-the-magazine season. The swells are the biggest and most consistent of the year, and outside the big-wave circus at Nazaré, most line-ups are wonderfully quiet.

    Be honest with yourself about your level, though. Much of the west coast gets heavy and serious in winter, and cold water plus wind makes for demanding sessions. The upside is remarkable: score a clean winter day at a quality reef with only a handful of surfers out and you'll understand why we keep coming back. Even if you're not paddling out, standing at the Nazaré lighthouse watching giants detonate is unforgettable.

    Average conditions

    • Water temperature: 13–16°C (55–61°F)
    • Air temperature: 11–17°C (52–63°F)
    • Recommended wetsuit: 4/3mm plus 3mm boots; a 5/3mm and gloves in the north for longer sessions

    Best regions

    • Nazaré (spectating; experts only in the water)
    • Peniche (Supertubos on the right day)
    • The Algarve's south coast (the mainland's mildest winter option)

Beginners still get manageable days, intermediates get consistency, and advanced surfers get the power building week by week. If you're a pure beginner set on the warmest, gentlest water, come in July; if you're an experienced surfer chasing size, target November to February.

    Portugal's water temperatures throughout the year

    Portugal's water is colder than most first-time visitors expect, and there's a good reason for it. The coast sits in the path of cooler North Atlantic currents, and in summer a phenomenon called upwelling — north winds pushing surface water offshore and drawing cold, deep water up in its place — can drop the sea several degrees, sometimes overnight. That's why the air can be 30°C while the sea sits at 16°C.

    The good news: with the right wetsuit, every season is comfortable. The table below is a fair reference for the central and Lisbon surf coasts, where most trips happen. Bear in mind the Algarve runs roughly 2–3°C warmer, and the far north runs a degree or two colder.

    Is the water cold?

    Compared to warm-water destinations, yes — but it's very manageable. Think of the Portuguese mainland as a step warmer than the UK, France or Ireland and a good deal warmer than Scandinavia. A 3/2mm suit covers most of the year on the central coast, and you'll only really want a 4/3mm and boots in the depths of winter. If you feel the cold, size up a millimetre — you'll last far longer in the water.

    Regional variation worth knowing

    The north–south spread is significant. The Algarve is the mainland's warm exception: the sheltered south coast around Tavira and the Ria Formosa can hit 22–25°C (72–77°F) in August and September, warm enough for boardshorts. The north (Porto, Viana do Castelo) is the coldest, rarely nudging much above 18–19°C even in its warmest month, and hit hardest by summer upwelling. The central coast and Lisbon sit comfortably in between. If warm water is a priority, head south; if you don't mind the chill and want empty waves, the north delivers.

    The best surf spots in Portugal

    Portugal's breaks come in every flavour — mellow sandy beach breaks, world-class reefs, heavy barrels and river-mouth points — so we'll work through it region by region. Here are the areas we know best, roughly north to south along the surf heartland, then out to the two ends of the country.

    Ericeira — Europe's first World Surfing Reserve

    Just 45 minutes north of Lisbon, Ericeira is a compact, whitewashed fishing town with an astonishing concentration of quality waves. In 2011 it became the first World Surfing Reserve in Europe, a status that protects a run of world-class reefs and points packed into around eight kilometres of coastline. It's our favourite base for a Portugal trip that leans intermediate-to-advanced, though there's plenty here for improvers too.

    Ribeira d'Ilhas

    The reserve's most famous and accessible wave: a long, peeling right-hand point that hosts WSL Qualifying Series contests. It works on all tides and a wide range of swell, with a long wall that gives multiple take-off zones — meaning the crowd spreads out and you can still get waves.

    Best for:

    • Intermediates and advanced surfers
    • Longboarders on the smaller, mellower days
    • Beginners on very small days (with a school)

    Foz do Lizandro

    A wide, sandy-bottomed beach at a river mouth five minutes south of town. This is where most surf schools bring their students — forgiving, gentle and consistent.

    Ideal for:

    • Beginners and first-timers
    • Longboarders
    • Families

    Coxos

    A world-class, powerful right point breaking over shallow reef — one of the heaviest waves in the reserve and a genuine bucket-list wave for those who can surf it. Be honest about your ability here.

    Best for:

    • Advanced surfers with solid tube experience only

    Who should visit Ericeira?

    • Intermediates ready to progress onto reefs and points
    • Advanced surfers after world-class, consistent waves
    • Beginners happy to learn at Foz do Lizandro and São Julião
    • Anyone who wants a proper surf town with great food and an easy Lisbon connection

    Peniche and the Silver Coast

    If Portugal has a surf capital, it's Peniche. This fishing town on a fishhook-shaped peninsula catches swell from almost every direction, which means there's nearly always something surfable somewhere. It's the beating heart of Portugal's surf-camp scene and, crucially, offers waves for absolute beginners and the world's best in the same 20-minute radius.

    Baleal

    A sweeping bay tucked behind Baleal island, sheltered from the biggest swells. The beach works like a sliding scale — head north for the gentlest waves, drift south for more size and power. It's one of Europe's genuinely great beginner zones, which is why the surf camps cluster here. See surf schools in Baleal.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and improvers
    • Longboarders
    • Families

    Supertubos

    The "Portuguese Pipeline" — a fast, hollow, barrelling beach break that hosts the World Surf League and graces plenty of magazine covers. It's powerful and unforgiving, best on a west swell and light offshore wind.

    Best for:

    • Advanced surfers and experienced barrel-riders only

    Nazaré (Praia do Norte)

    Forty minutes north, Nazaré needs little introduction: the underwater canyon here produces the biggest rideable waves on the planet. For all but a tiny handful of big-wave specialists, this is a spot to watch rather than surf — head to the Forte de São Miguel Arcanjo lighthouse between October and March for the show. On smaller days the town beach itself offers more normal surf.

    Best for:

    • Big-wave experts (Praia do Norte)
    • Everyone else as spectators

    Who should visit Peniche and the Silver Coast?

    • Beginners wanting the best learning beaches and surf camps in the country
    • Advanced surfers chasing barrels at Supertubos
    • Anyone who wants to witness Nazaré's giants
    • Surfers who value consistency — something's nearly always working here

    The Lisbon coast

    You can be surfing within half an hour of stepping off a plane at Lisbon, which makes the capital's coast unbeatable for a city-break surf trip or a first taste of Portugal. The waves split across two stretches: the Cascais line to the west of the city, and Costa da Caparica to the south across the river.

    Carcavelos

    The closest beach to central Lisbon and reachable by train in about 20 minutes. A consistent, sandy beach break that works across a wide range of conditions and hosts a lively surf scene with plenty of schools. It's the obvious first stop. See surf schools in Carcavelos.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and intermediates
    • Surfers without a car

    Costa da Caparica

    A long stretch of coast south of the city with dozens of peaks over more than 20 kilometres of sand — so you can nearly always find a bank that's working and spread away from the crowds. A favourite of locals and surf camps alike. See surf schools in Costa da Caparica.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and improvers
    • Anyone wanting variety and space

    Guincho

    Near Cascais and fully exposed to the Atlantic, Guincho brings power — and wind. It's a beautiful, wild beach that's rewarded experienced surfers and hosted world championships, but the currents and gusts make it one for confident surfers, not first lessons.

    Best for:

    • Intermediate and advanced surfers
    • Windsurfers and kitesurfers

    Who should visit the Lisbon coast?

    • Beginners wanting the easiest access in the country
    • City-break travellers who want to combine surf and Lisbon
    • Surfers relying on public transport
    • Anyone chasing variety across a short, well-connected coast

    The Algarve

    Portugal's sunny south has a secret weapon: two coasts facing different directions, so there's almost always a sheltered option whatever the wind and swell are doing. The wild, west-facing Costa Vicentina runs north from Sagres and takes the full Atlantic — powerful, uncrowded and dramatic. The south coast around Lagos and Sagres is more sheltered, warmer and gentler, and comes into its own in summer when the west goes flat. It's also the mainland's warm-water region.

    Praia do Amado

    One of the Costa Vicentina's most consistent and beginner-friendly beaches, with schools on the sand and forgiving waves for every level. A lovely, wild setting inside a natural park.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and intermediates
    • Surf-school lessons

    Praia da Arrifana

    Set beneath dramatic cliffs near Aljezur, Arrifana offers a sheltered bay that's great for learning plus a punchy right-hand point for the more experienced. One of the best all-round spots on the west coast.

    Ideal for:

    • Beginners in the bay
    • Intermediates and advanced surfers on the point

    Sagres (Tonel, Beliche, Mareta)

    The peninsula at Portugal's south-western tip catches both west and south swells, making it the region's most reliable surf hub. Tonel and Beliche face the Atlantic; sheltered Mareta is a gentle south-facing option when everywhere else is too big. See surf schools in Sagres.

    Best for:

    • All levels, depending on which beach and the conditions
    • Wave-hunters who like options within a short drive

    Who should visit the Algarve?

    • Beginners wanting warm water and sheltered summer beaches
    • Intermediate and advanced surfers chasing the wild Costa Vicentina in autumn and winter
    • Anyone combining surf with a proper holiday
    • Surfers who want the flexibility of two coasts

    The North (Porto, the Silver Coast and Costa Verde)

    The north is Portugal's wild, cold, uncrowded corner, and we adore it. The water's colder, the surf's a touch wilder and less predictable, and the line-ups are the emptiest on the mainland. Fly into Porto and you can surf within 20 minutes at Matosinhos, then explore the untamed beaches stretching south to Espinho and north towards the Spanish border.

    Matosinhos

    Porto's main city beach and one of the best places in Europe to learn. A big harbour wall shelters it from the worst of the north wind, so it's often surfable when the rest of the coast is stormy — and it's barely 20 minutes from the airport. See surf schools in Matosinhos.

    Best for:

    • Beginners and improvers
    • Quick weekend or city-break surf trips

    Espinho

    The north's best-known surf town, 20 kilometres south of Porto, with easy car and train access and a punchy beach break that hosts pro-junior and longboard events. Much of the beach works for beginners on smaller days.

    Best for:

    • Beginners to intermediates
    • Surfers based in Porto without a car

    Viana do Castelo (Cabedelo)

    A handsome town up towards the Spanish border, with the Cabedelo beach break sheltered by a long pier and running consistently much of the year. A strong local scene and a magnet for wind- and kitesurfers too.

    Best for:

    • Improving beginners and intermediates
    • Anyone wanting empty waves and authentic surroundings

    Who should visit the North?

    • Beginners learning at Matosinhos or Espinho
    • Intermediates who want space and don't mind cold water
    • City-break surfers using Porto as a base
    • Anyone tired of crowds and after a more authentic, wild trip

    Our personal favourite

    We keep coming back to Ericeira — the sheer density of quality waves in a walkable town, the easy Lisbon connection, and the fact you can surf a mellow beach break in the morning and watch the pros at Coxos in the afternoon. The honest trade-off is crowds: the reserve's fame means the best spots get busy, and the reefs aren't beginner terrain. If we wanted warm water and a holiday feel, we'd pick the Algarve for its two-coast flexibility; if we wanted solitude, we'd point the car north. But for an all-round Portugal trip, Ericeira is the one.

    The best beginner surf spots in Portugal

    Portugal is one of the best places in the world to learn to surf. Long sandy beaches, gentle summer swells, warm-ish water and a mature surf-school scene mean there's a forgiving wave for almost anyone. These are the spots we'd send a first-timer to.

    1. Baleal (Peniche)

    Best for: absolute beginners and first lessons.

    The gold standard for learning in Portugal. Sheltered, sandy and gentle, with the bay working as a sliding scale of size, plus more surf camps and schools than almost anywhere else in the country. Browse Baleal surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Sheltered from the biggest swells
    • Sandy bottom and gentle, rolling waves
    • Huge choice of surf schools and camps
    • Consistent enough to surf most days

    2. Carcavelos (Lisbon)

    Best for: beginners who want easy access from the city.

    The most accessible beginner beach in the country — a 20-minute train ride from central Lisbon, a wide sandy break, and schools right on the sand. See Carcavelos surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Reachable without a car
    • Consistent, forgiving beach break
    • Loads of schools and rentals
    • Perfect for a city-break first session

    3. Costa da Caparica (Lisbon)

    Best for: beginners wanting space and variety.

    A vast stretch of beach with dozens of peaks, so you can always find a quieter, mellower bank away from the crowd. See Costa da Caparica surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Long, sandy and consistent
    • Plenty of room to spread out
    • Strong surf-camp scene
    • Beginner-friendly on the right banks, especially early morning

    4. Foz do Lizandro (Ericeira)

    Best for: beginners who also want a great surf town.

    A sandy river-mouth beach that's the go-to teaching spot in the World Surfing Reserve — gentle waves with world-class breaks a short drive away for when you progress. See Ericeira surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Sandy bottom and forgiving waves
    • Surf schools on the beach
    • Room to progress within the reserve
    • Lovely town on the doorstep

    5. Praia do Amado (Algarve)

    Best for: beginners wanting warm water and a wild setting.

    One of the Costa Vicentina's most consistent and forgiving beaches, with schools on the sand, a great beach bar and a stunning natural-park backdrop.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Consistent, beginner-friendly beach breaks
    • Warmer water than the central and north coasts
    • Schools and rentals on site
    • Unspoiled, uncrowded surroundings

    6. Matosinhos (Porto)

    Best for: beginners on a Porto city break.

    Porto's main learning beach, sheltered by a harbour wall and just 20 minutes from the airport — often surfable when the rest of the north is blown out. See Matosinhos surf schools.

    Why we recommend it:

    • Sheltered from the north wind
    • Very easy access from the city
    • Established schools and rentals
    • Great for a quick weekend trip

    What makes a good beginner surf beach?

    When we're judging a beach for first-timers, we look for a few things: a sandy bottom (no reef or rocks to worry about), gentle, rolling waves rather than steep or hollow ones, consistency so you're not sitting waiting for sets, space to keep clear of other surfers, and a good choice of reputable surf schools with certified instructors. The beaches above tick all of those boxes.

    Should beginners surf in peak season?

    Summer (June to August) is the easiest time to learn: the waves are at their smallest and most forgiving and the water's at its warmest. The trade-off is crowds — the popular beginner beaches fill up, especially with surf-school groups in the mornings. Our advice: come in summer if it's your first time, but surf early. A dawn lesson before the wind and the crowds arrive is calmer, cleaner and far more enjoyable. Late spring and September are lovely quieter alternatives if you've got a little experience.

    Portugal surf trip packing list

    Built from years of our own trips — here's what actually earns its place in the board bag.

    🏄 Surf essentials

    Surfboard

    If you're bringing your own, match it to the season. Summer's small, playful waves suit a groveller, fish or longboard; autumn and winter's power calls for a step-up or your regular shortboard. Beginners are usually better off renting a soft-top locally — schools and shops in every surf town rent boards cheaply, saving you airline board fees.

    Wetsuit

    The single most important item. A 3/2mm covers most of the year on the central coast; bring a 4/3mm and 3mm boots for winter and early spring, or a 5/3mm and gloves if you're heading to the cold north in the depths of winter. In the Algarve in high summer, a shorty or even boardshorts will do.

    Surf wax

    Match it to the water temperature — cool wax for winter and spring, cool-to-warm for autumn, warm for high summer, especially in the Algarve. Bring a bit more than you think; wax is easy to forget and annoying to hunt down.

    Changing poncho / towel

    Invaluable for getting changed in car parks and on windy beaches, and much warmer than a normal towel between sessions on cold days.

    Useful extras

    • Reusable water bottle
    • Dry bag for wet wetsuits
    • Waterproof phone pouch
    • Small first aid kit
    • Earplugs (surfer's ear is a real risk in cold Atlantic water)
    • Ding repair kit
    • Flip-flops / sandals
    • Snorkel and mask for flat days

    Here's what years of trips here have taught us: the best surf trip to Portugal isn't the one with the biggest waves. It's the one where you stay flexible — chase the clean corner when the wind swings, take the lesson when it's small, watch the giants when it's huge, and fill the flat days with long lunches, coast roads and cold swims. Come with a plan, but hold it loosely. Portugal always has a wave for you somewhere.

    FAQs

    Is Portugal good for beginner surfers?

    Yes — it's one of the best in the world. Long sandy beaches, gentle summer swells and a huge surf-school scene make places like Baleal, Carcavelos, Costa da Caparica and Praia do Amado ideal for learning. Come in summer for the easiest conditions.

    What is the best time of year to surf in Portugal?

    It depends on your level. Beginners should come in summer for small, warm, forgiving waves. Advanced surfers want autumn and winter for size and power. For the best all-round balance, we'd pick September and October — warm water, building swells and thinner crowds.

    Can you surf in Portugal all year round?

    Absolutely. Portugal picks up Atlantic swell in every season — it's one of the most consistent coasts in Europe, with surfable waves on the majority of days somewhere along the coast.

    Do I need a wetsuit in Portugal?

    Almost always, yes. The Atlantic here is colder than many expect. A 3/2mm suits most of the year on the central coast, with a 4/3mm and boots for winter. The Algarve in high summer is the one exception, where a shorty or boardshorts can be enough.

    Is the Algarve or the north better for surfing?

    Different trips entirely. The Algarve has the warmest water, sheltered beginner beaches and a holiday feel, plus wild west-coast waves in winter. The north is colder and wilder, with the emptiest line-ups on the mainland. Choose the Algarve for warmth and comfort, the north for solitude.

    Is Ericeira good for surfing?

    Very — it's Europe's first World Surfing Reserve, with a remarkable density of world-class reefs and points in a small area, plus gentle learning beaches nearby. It leans intermediate-to-advanced but has something for everyone.

    Can you longboard in Portugal?

    Yes. There are plenty of long, peeling walls for logging, especially on the smaller days — Ribeira d'Ilhas in Ericeira, the mellower banks at Costa da Caparica and Carcavelos, and countless summer beach breaks all suit longboarders well.

    Is surfing in Portugal expensive?

    No — it's excellent value by Western-European standards. Board and wetsuit rental typically runs around €20–30 per day, lessons and surf camps are affordable, and food and accommodation are cheap. A fresh seafood meal for around €15 is normal.

    Is Portugal a good destination for a surf road trip?

    It's one of the best. The coast is compact, easy to drive, and packed with variety — you can surf a beginner beach, a world-class reef and a big-wave spectacle within a couple of hours' drive. Rent a car, follow the forecast, and let the wind decide where you surf each day.
